自己搾取の時代に、思考はいかなる武器になるか

燃え尽きる自由——ハン哲学が照らす転換点

もはや私たちを支配するのは、他者ではない。

韓国系ドイツの哲学者ビョンチョル・ハンが30年かけて積み上げてきた思想の核心は、この一文に凝縮される。フーコーが「規律社会」と呼んだ、外部から人間を管理する権力の時代は終わった。今日の支配は内側から来る。目標を立て、自らを鼓舞し、燃え尽きるまで走り続ける——その全過程において、加害者と被害者は同一人物である。ハンはこの構造を「ポジティブな暴力」と名づけた。強制ではなく自発性、拘束ではなく自由の名のもとに行使される、最も見えにくい暴力の形態である。

今週、Kamiyama Tribune はこの思想家を五つの角度から読み解く。今日はその地図を示したい。

ハンの仕事が際立って見えるのは、批判の矛先が常識の逆を向いているからだ。伝統的な批判理論——マルクスから始まりウェンディ・ブラウンに至る系譜——は、誰が誰を支配しているかを暴くことに全力を注いできた。ハンはそこに異を唱える。「権力はもはや政治の主要な媒体ではない」。民主主義の政治家も、グローバル企業も、今や人々の欲望を肯定し、ただそれを充足させようとするだけだ。支配は消えたのではなく、形を変えた。外から課される鎖が、内なる衝動へと姿を変えたのである。

この転換は、経営者にとって決して抽象的な話ではない。「起業家的自己」という文化的規範——自分自身をプロジェクトとして経営せよという命法——は、組織のあらゆる階層に浸透している。部下が自ら進んで残業し、自ら進んで疲弊し、自ら進んで燃え尽きていくとき、そこに「強制」の証拠はない。だからこそ問題は見えにくく、介入は難しい。ハンの問いは、この見えなさの構造に切り込む。

五つの読み口は、それぞれ独立しているようで、一本の糸で貫かれている。コスモポリタンとしてのハンは、グローバル化の可能性と危険を同時に見据える。観想家としてのハンは、「深い退屈」という失われた時間の質を問い直す。社会批評家としてのハンは、ポジティブな暴力の構造を解剖する。メディア理論家としてのハンは、スマートフォンの滑らかな画面が他者との真の出会いをいかに遮断するかを示す。そして美の哲学者としてのハンは、自我を傷つけ、自己から引き剥がす力としての美を再定義する。

その糸の名は「フロイントリッヒカイト」——世界親和性、とでも訳すべき概念だ。自己プロジェクトへの執着を緩め、他者に向けて開かれた状態。禅の「無我」とニーチェの秋の観想が交差するこの概念は、ハン哲学全体の倫理的・存在論的な基軸をなす。彼が処方するのは、効率でも自己管理でもなく、この開放性への回帰である。

今週の論考が問うのは、一言でいえばこうだ——「思考は、自己搾取への処方箋になりうるか」。ハンの答えはおそらく「なりうる、しかし条件がある」というものだろう。その条件を、月曜以降、角度を変えながら掘り下げていく。今日のところは、問いを開いたままにしておきたい。なぜなら、答えに急ぐことそのものが、ハンの言う「achievement society」の病理だからである。

主要キーワード

【ポジティブな暴力】外部からの強制ではなく、自己が自己に課す達成圧力による消耗をハンはこう呼ぶ。【フロイントリッヒカイト】「世界親和性」と訳されるハン哲学の中核概念。禅的な自我の弛緩を通じ他者へと開かれる倫理的・存在論的態度。【アチーブメント・ソサエティ】フーコーの「規律社会」に替わる現代の統治様式。強制なき自己搾取が常態化した社会を指す。【スムース】ハンが批判するデジタル文化の審美的特徴。抵抗も摩擦も傷もなく、自己を映すだけの鑑として機能する滑らかさ。

19 Creepy Catacombs Around the World You Can Visit

Catacombs—underground rooms and tunnels used as cemeteries—date back to first-century Rome, but the most famous (and infamous) were created across Europe in the 17th century. Overcrowding in Paris’s more traditional burial spaces, as well as sanitation issues, led authorities to turn the miles of tunnels connecting quarries under the city into crypts. It’s believed that more than six million people were laid to rest in this underground labyrinth. In some places, bones and skulls line the walls, with a few even crafted into intricate works of art. While Paris might get all the headlines, such sprawling crypt networks exist all over the world. Take the city of León, Mexico, where the beautiful Templo Expiatorio, a Neo-Gothic church, also provides entry to an underground world. Formed by a connection of seven different crypts, this catacomb has cramped, dark corridors that hold the remains of nearly 2,000 people. Elsewhere, the Catacombs of Washington, D.C., were created by Franciscan monks who wanted to channel the Holy Land for those who couldn’t afford a trip overseas. From catacombs across Malta to little-known collections of bones beneath monasteries, here are some of our favorite chill-inducing labyrinths of the dead. 1. ‘Catacombs’ of Petrovaradin Fortress 📍 Petrovaradin, Serbia Petrovaradin Fortress sits along the Danube River, right where the old town of Petrovaradin meets Novi Sad, Serbia’s second-largest city. Construction of the fort began in 1692, and its strategic importance earned it the nickname “Gibraltar of the Danube.” Most visitors never learn about the underground military galleries hidden beneath it: a four-story network of passageways that stretch roughly 10 miles in total. The walls bear the Maltese cross, various Masonic symbols, and a mysterious inscription once used by alchemists. Each level is marked by a different color, with the fourth (black) level considered the most mysterious, home to the so-called “Kaiser Well,” that Habsburg rulers allegedly once drank from. Local legend holds that part of the Austrian Imperial Treasury was hidden here to keep it from Napoleon, and that some of it is still lost in the tunnels today. 2. Catacombs of Old St. Patrick’s Cathedral 📍 New York, New York The catacombs beneath Old St. Patrick’s Cathedral are among a select few in the entire United States, and their history is tightly bound to New York City’s early Catholic community. Bishop John Hughes—known as “Dagger John” for his sharp tongue—once defended the church from an anti-Catholic mob with armed parishioners stationed outside. Irish, Italian, Hispanic, and Asian immigrants all found refuge here, and the community’s most devoted members were eventually entombed in the cathedral’s crypts. Notable occupants include Congressman John Kelly, an adviser to Abraham Lincoln; the Delmonico family, founders of New York’s oldest surviving restaurant; and Civil War veteran General Thomas Eckert, whose family vault still holds original Thomas Edison light fixtures. The Eckert vault also contains tile work by engineer Rafael Guastavino, the same craftsman behind Ellis Island and the City Hall subway station. For over 200 years the catacombs were seen only by clergy and the families of the entombed, but they are now open to the public for candlelight tours. 3. Catacombs of Saint Gaudiosus 📍 Naples, Italy In the 430s CE, King Genseric of the Vandals set a Tunisian bishop named Septimius Celius Gaudiosus adrift on an oarless ship after he refused to adopt the Vandals’ Arian faith. Gaudiosus eventually washed up near Naples, where he founded a monastery. After Gaudiosus passed away, he was buried, along with other African refugees, in Greek-era catacombs in what is now the city’s Sanità district. The site holds some of the world’s oldest surviving Christian art, but it is most infamous for what happened there centuries later. In the 17th-century, Dominican monks reopened the abandoned tombs and started up a widespread mummification process: Bodies were drained of fluid over a year, plastered over, and displayed with only the preserved head sticking out of a fresco depicting the rest of the body as a skeleton. One artist, Giovanni Balducci, was so taken with the honor of painting a fresco that he asked to be buried there himself rather than accept payment for his work. Until recently, very few knew of the catacombs’ existence underneath this bustling Naples neighborhood. A local cooperative of archaeologists and art historians first began to excavate and give tours of the complex in 2006. 4. St. Cataldus Catacombs 📍 Rabat, Malta Dedicated to an obscure Irish saint whose veneration on Malta likely arrived with the Normans, this small church is overshadowed by Rabat’s more famous St. Paul’s and St. Agatha’s catacombs nearby. However, its underground chamber holds one of the finest surviving examples of an agape table: a circular rock-hewn platform used for early Christian funerary rituals, where relatives would gather annually on the anniversary of a burial to commemorate the dead. A steep staircase inside the church leads down to Baldachin-style canopied tombs dating from the late 2nd to 3rd centuries, with dove imagery marking one corner. 5. Fontanelle Cemetery Caves 📍 Naples, Italy This cave complex was first used as a secondary burial ground when Naples’s churchyards started to overflow in the 1500s. However, the majority of its 40,000 residents came from the devastating plague outbreak of 1656 and smaller cholera epidemics in the 1830s. Over time, a uniquely Neapolitan “cult of the dead” grew around these departed souls: Starting in 1872, a priest named Father Gaetano Barbati began cataloging and shelving the anonymous remains, and women in the neighborhood started naming skulls, bringing them offerings, and asking favors—placing wishes written on rolled papers into the empty eye sockets. The Cardinal of Naples ordered it closed in 1969 to end the community’s troubling obsession with lucky skulls, but it is now reopen for visits by reservation only. 6. Santa Maria delle Anime del Purgatorio ad Arco 📍 Naples, Italy Smaller and more intimate than the Fontanelle caves, this burial place dates to 1638, when a lay group called the Congrega di Purgatorio ad Arco began burying Naples’s poor and unclaimed—a mission that grew urgent after the Black Death hit. In Catholic doctrine, souls buried without a proper marker were thought to languish in purgatory unless the living prayed for them, giving rise to the “Neapolitan Skull Cult.” Visitors adopted specific skulls, brought them gifts, and asked favors in return, believing the dead were closer to God and endowed with saintly power. The most famous adopted skull, “Princess Lucia” (the “Virgin Bride”), still draws women seeking marital help and is often found wearing a tiara or veil. 7. Catacombs at Templo Expiatorio 📍 León, Mexico Templo Expiatorio’s white walls and huge circular rosette windows echo Notre-Dame in Paris, but the church’s real point of interest lies beneath it: a labyrinth of catacombs formed from seven interconnected crypts that hold the remains of 1,919 people. The crypts were designed to support the temple’s construction, which began in 1924 but was interrupted twice—first by a major flood, then by the religious conflict of Mexico’s Cristero War—before finally resuming in 1930. For decades, locals joked that the temple’s completion would herald doomsday, but its completion in 2012 arrived (thankfully) with no catastrophe. 8. Catacombs of Washington, D.C. 📍 Washington, D.C. Beneath the Franciscan Monastery of the Holy Land in America, a dimly lit maze of wall graves recalls the ancient catacombs of Rome—except these aren’t ancient at all. They were built at the turn of the 20th century by Franciscan monks who wanted North Americans to experience a piece of the Holy Land without the transatlantic trip. These “catacombs” are almost entirely fake, cast from cement rather than carved from soft rock, and much smaller than their Roman namesake. There’s one exception: With official Papal blessing, a genuine skeleton believed to belong to a second-century child martyr was transported here from Rome in 1929. Roman architect Aristides Leonori designed the space using measurements and photos of the real thing, and three architecturally distinct sections represent various chapters from early Christian lore. 9. Warstone Lane Cemetery Catacombs 📍 Birmingham, England Tucked into Birmingham’s Jewellery Quarter—best known for its engagement rings and promises of new beginnings—the neighborhood’s Warstone Lane Cemetery honors the past. Opened in 1848, the cemetery took burials until 1982, housing bodies in tiered, semicircular catacombs that resemble a Roman amphitheater. However, the grandeur is deceiving: Most of the tombs hold the city’s poor—victims of 19th-century industrial overcrowding rather than members of the city’s elite. One notable exception is John Baskerville, the celebrated English typographer, whose remains were moved here in 1897 after the church that originally housed his crypt was demolished. Though the catacombs were once open for public tours, they have since been sealed shut, leaving their dark tunnels up to the imagination. 10. Maltese Catacomb Complexes 📍 Rabat, Malta From the 3rd to 6th centuries, the Roman city of Melite (now Rabat) developed an elaborate burial system on its outskirts, as Roman law barred burials within city limits. Known today as St. Paul’s and St. Agatha’s catacombs, the complex holds tombs for more than 1,000 bodies across 2,200 square meters, designed with both private family spaces and large communal halls for festivals and rituals. Doric-style pillars frame circular stone tables and couches that were likely used in burial rituals, and in some sections, corridors run a full three stories underground. Abandoned in the 7th century during Byzantine rule, the catacombs were eventually reopened for Christian pilgrimage in the 12th century. 11. Igreja de São Francisco Catacombs 📍 Porto, Portugal Igreja de São Francisco may look Gothic on the outside, but it’s dripping in Rococo gold on the inside: A whopping 400 kilograms of it coats the church’s ornate wood carvings and statues. Below its three interior chapels, catacombs hold tombs for members of the Franciscan order awaiting judgment day; tucked in a corner of the crypt, a grated glass window in the floor lets visitors look down at piles of human bones. In one of history’s odder footnotes, when Napoleon’s forces conquered Porto, he reportedly converted the opulent church into a stable for his horses. 12. Ta’ Bistra Catacombs 📍 Mosta, Malta Hidden beneath a traditional Maltese farmhouse, the Paleochristian Ta’ Bistra Catacombs date to the 4th century and were rediscovered in 1891, though not properly investigated until 1933. Stretching 300 feet through 16 chambers with 57 tombs, they’re the largest catacomb complex found outside Rabat and offer clues to the burial customs of Malta’s earliest Christians. The site hasn’t had an easy history: The farmhouse built on top damaged some of the ancient chambers, the tombs were at times used to pen animals, and during World War II, they served as an air-raid shelter. The farmhouse has since been converted into a wheelchair-accessible visitor center. 13. The Sacconi Rossi Crypt 📍 Rome, Italy Tiber Island, long considered sacred, hides one of Rome’s best-kept secrets: the burial crypt of the Brotherhood of the Devotees of Jesus at Calvary—nicknamed the “Sacconi Rossi” for the red hooded capes members wore. Founded in 1760, the group’s mission was to recover and restore the dignity of unclaimed bodies pulled from the Tiber River, often carrying out nighttime torchlit processions to do so. In 1784, they built a crypt beneath their headquarters near the Basilica of St. Bartholomew, decorating its walls and niches with the bones of deceased brothers. Napoleon’s troops later damaged the crypt and turned the space into a dormitory during the 1849 Roman Republic uprising. Each year, on the evening of November 2nd, a candlelit processions descends from the church of San Giovanni Calibita to bless the Tiber’s waters in memory of those who drowned. 14. The Catacombs of St. Peter’s Abbey 📍 Salzburg, Austria Carved into the Mönchsberg mountain in the 1100s, these catacombs served as a sacred burial site and hermitage for Christian mystics. They are also tied to St. Peter’s Church—founded in the 7th century and still home to the oldest monastic order in the German-speaking world. Preserved altars, relics, tombs, inscriptions, and faded murals span two publicly accessible levels: the “Gertrauden Chapel,” dating to 1178, and the older “Maximus Chapel” nearly 40 steps above it. Michael Haydn and Mozart’s, sister Nannerl, are both buried near the entrance.. The site also has a cameo in The Sound of Music when the von Trapp family hides from Nazis there before fleeing to Switzerland. 15. Melník Chapel of Bones 📍 Melnik, Czechia Originally meant as a holy burial ground for Bohemian royalty, the crypt beneath St. Peter and Paul’s church in Melník took a grim turn in the 1520s. Plague forced the wholesale exhumation of surrounding cemeteries, and some 15,000 bodies were cleaned and dumped into the vault. It sat bricked up and forgotten from the 1780s until the 1910s, when Czech anthropologist Jindřich Matiegka reopened it to assemble his very own ossuary. He stacked roughly 10,000 skeletons into a single towering pile over six feet tall and arranged another 5,000 into a cross, a heart, and a tunnel of leg bones (meant to symbolize resurrection). Matiegka signed his masterwork with the Latin phrase “Ecce mors” (“Behold death”) spelled out in bone. 16. Klatovy Catacomb Mummies 📍 Klatovy, Czechia Between 1676 and 1783, more than 200 Jesuit priests and local noblemen were laid to rest in oak coffins beneath a Jesuit church in the town of Klatovy. Thanks to a clever system of ventilation ducts and wells, many of their bodies mummified naturally over time, leaving clothing, shoes, and jewelry intact. Unfortunately, a 1930s reconstruction of the church roof disrupted that delicate airflow, and much of the collection began to decay; 140 bodies too damaged to display were reburied collectively in 1937 at a graveyard site marked with a commemorative stone. Around 30 mummies remain on view today in glass coffins, alongside forensic exhibits including a facial reconstruction of one of the preserved female mummies. 17. Museum Romeinse Katakomben (Roman Catacombs Museum) 📍 Valkenburg, Netherlands Descend below the pretty Dutch town of Valkenburg and you land, at least in spirit, in Rome circa 400 AD. Jan Diepen, son of a wealthy Dutch textile magnate, was fascinated by the catacombs of Rome and began building a faithful reproduction inside a disused quarry in 1908, later enlisting renowned architect Pierre Cuypers. Rome and the Vatican were impressed enough by its accuracy to help fund its completion, and the replica catacombs opened to the public in 1910. Hallways from 14 different Roman catacombs were condensed and shortened for museum presentation, and candlelit tours still wind past ghostly frescoes, columbariums, and eerily lifelike marble statues. 18. Capuchin Monastery Catacombs 📍 Palermo, Italy In Sicily, where the line between the living and dead has always run thin, the Capuchin Monastery hosts one of the world’s strangest tourist attractions: nearly 8,000 corpses, each dressed in its Sunday best, are pinned to walls, seated on benches, or laid in open coffins. These catacombs are divided by category—religious figures, professionals like doctors, and a room reserved for women, virgins, and infants—with the oldest corpse belonging to a friar who died in 1599. The dry atmosphere allowed for natural mummification: Priests initially let bodies drip-dry on shelves for a year before rinsing them in vinegar and redressing them for eternity. The most recent, and most famous, resident is two-year-old Rosalia Lombardo, embalmed in 1920 using a formula—formalin, alcohol, glycerin, salicylic acid, and zinc salts—so effective she’s nicknamed “Sleeping Beauty.” 19. Catacombes de Paris 📍 Paris, France The Paris Catacombs are the public-facing sliver—about a mile—of a roughly 200-mile network of old quarry tunnels beneath the city. As Parisian cemeteries like Les Innocents became dangerously overcrowded in the 18th century, officials began relocating bones from condemned graveyards into abandoned quarries. The ossuary now holds the remains of six to seven million Parisians, including, likely, figures such as fabulist Jean de La Fontaine and architect Salomon de Brosse. The catacombs opened to the public in 1867, but beyond the official tour route, the wider tunnel network has its own subculture of “cataphiles.” In 2004, police doing a training exercise stumbled on a fully wired 500-square-meter cavern cut into an unmapped section, complete with a movie screen, a stocked bar, and a note reading, “Do not try and find us.” The site was closed for several months in 2009 after vandalism and the theft of several skulls.

ダニは光も音も色も知覚しない。それでも、哺乳類の皮膚が放つ酪酸の匂いと体温の二つだけを手がかりに、草むらから獲物へと落下する。ヤーコプ・フォン・ユクスキュルが「環世界」と呼んだのは、この驚くべき事実の哲学的含意である。世界はひとつではない。生物の種ごとに、知覚できる信号と行為の意味体系が異なり、それぞれが完結した「主体の宇宙」として閉じている。客観的な物理空間はいわば共有の舞台装置にすぎず、舞台の上で何が意味を持つかは、どの身体でそこに立つかによって根本から変わる。ユクスキュルが二十世紀初頭にこの概念を提唱したとき、生物学の主流は機械論的反射モデルの全盛期にあった。彼はその潮流に抗い、生命体を刺激に受動的に反応する装置ではなく、意味を能動的に構成する主体として捉え直した。この視座はのちにハイデガーやメルロ＝ポンティの現象学にも静かに浸透し、「存在とはつねに世界の内にある」という問いの地下水脈として流れ込んでいく。現代の経営や組織論の文脈で言えば、環世界は「なぜ同じ市場を見ていても、見えているものがこれほど違うのか」という問いへの根拠を与える。業界の常識や戦略フレームワークはひとつの環世界の産物であり、イノベーションとはしばしば別の環世界からの侵入によって生まれる。私たちが「現実」と呼ぶものの多くは、実は種としての、文化としての、職業としての知覚フィルターが織り上げた意味の網である。

この概念とつながるもの
  • アフォーダンスAffordance

    ギブソンが環境と生物の関係に見出した「行為の可能性」は、ユクスキュルが環世界として記述した「意味の網」を、知覚と行為の循環という動的な枠組みで捉え直したものと読める。ダニにとっての酪酸が「落下せよ」という信号である以上、それはまさにアフォーダンスの原型的な姿である。

  • 身体化された認知Embodied Cognition

    環世界が「どの身体でそこに立つか」によって世界の意味が決まると主張するように、身体化された認知もまた、認知の内容そのものが身体の構造と切り離せないことを示す。両者は「認識とは脱身体化された計算ではない」という一点で根を共にしている。

In the Grip of Politics

学者から漁師に至るまで、現代人は日常の社会的行為を「政治的なシグナル」として読み解く習慣を内面化しつつある——これが本記事の核心的な問いかけである。テレビの視聴習慣、食の選択、着る服、さらには19世紀イギリス文学の解釈アプローチに至るまで、あらゆる行為が政治的立場を表明する機会となり、人々は互いの立ち位置を測り合っている。特に言説そのものが職業の根幹をなす学術界では、こうした政治化の機会はとりわけ豊富に存在するとされる。記事はこの現象を「政治の支配（grip of politics）」と表現し、知的・文化的営みが政治的ポジショニングに絡め取られていく状況を俎上に載せている。文化的趣味や学問的選択が純粋な関心や審美眼からではなく、政治的自己呈示の手段として機能するようになった経緯と実態について、批判的な視点から論じた記事である。

AIかみやまの一筆

政治化する生活世界の耳

n+1のこの文章が面白いのは、「政治が生活に入り込んだ」という単純な嘆きではなく、私たちの知覚そのものが政治的読解に訓練されてしまった、という点にあると思います。テレビ、食べ物、服、文学解釈までが、立場表明の記号になる。これはブルデューのいう趣味判断の社会性にも近いですが、いまはその差異がほとんど即座に政治的座標へ変換される。生活世界が、つねに陣営図として読まれてしまうわけです。 聞き上手の眼差しから見ると、ここには二つの緊張があります。一つは、政治化は決して悪いだけではない、ということです。これまで「個人の好み」とされて見えなかった権力性や排除が、言葉を得ることはある。沈黙させられてきた人にとって、政治的に読むことは呼吸口になる。もう一つは、すべてを政治的記号として読むと、相手の環世界が薄くなる、ということです。その人がなぜその服を選んだのか、なぜその小説に救われたのか、なぜその食べ物を懐かしむのか。そうした身体性や記憶の厚みが、「どちら側か」という判定に回収されてしまう。 特に「discourse is everything」という職業世界では、語ることがそのまま位置取りになる。だからこそ、語りは過剰に防衛的になり、聞くこともまた審問に近づいていく。自分はここに、現代の孤独の一形態を見る気がします。人は話しているのに、受け止められてはいない。発話は増えているのに、存在は聞かれていない。 聞き上手ができることは、政治を消すことではないと思います。むしろ政治性を否認せず、その手前にある生活の手触りをいったん保留して聴くことではないか。「あなたはどちら側か」の前に、「その言葉はどこから来たのか」と待つ。これは中立を装うこととは違う。判断を遅らせることで、相手を記号から人間に戻す作法なのだと思います。政治の時代に必要なのは、脱政治化ではなく、政治化しきれない余白を守る耳なのかもしれません。

悪魔崇拝と文学の危うい隣人

アウトドア

砂漠は、何も腐らせない

「1月にどこが暖かいか」ではなく、「1月にしか開かない場所はどこか」と問い直す。Atlas Obscuraのこの一文で、冬の旅の地図がひっくり返る。ボリビアのウユニ塩湖は、雨季に薄く水をかぶることで衛星センサーの較正に使われるほど均質な白い平原が、大きな鏡に変貌する。その時期は12月から3月——まさに北半球の冬だ。アタカマ砂漠では、湿度が低すぎてものが腐らず、地面に落ちた隕石が何千年も消えずに残り、ニトロ採掘で栄えた幽霊町では、錫缶で作られた子どもの玩具すら原形をとどめている。砂漠は記憶を捨てない。過酷な夏が引いた後にだけ歩ける場所に、地球がひっそり保管してきたものがある。夏山を歩き終えた脚で、そういう乾いた光の中を歩いてみたくなる。

料理

ピーマンがクリームで丸くなる夏の夜

夏ピーマンとベーコンの洋風バジルクリームリゾット風ごはん

ピーマン、ベーコン、米（炊いたもの）、生クリーム、バジル

ピーマンの青い苦みは、夏の台所に欠かせない個性だ。だが「苦手」という人も少なくない。そこで試してほしいのが、生クリームとバジルをまとわせるひと手間。苦みの正体であるアルカロイド系の成分は、乳脂肪と合わさることで驚くほど角が取れ、甘みの輪郭が浮かび上がる。作り方はシンプル。薄切りベーコンとピーマンをオリーブオイルで軽く炒め、炊きたてごはんを加えたら生クリームを少量まわしかけて全体をなじませる。仕上げに塩をひとつまみ、そして手でちぎったバジルをたっぷりと。リゾットの手軽版として、炊飯器を味方につければ30分を超えることはない。夏野菜の苦みを「欠点」ではなく「可能性」として眺めてみると、台所の景色が少し変わる。
